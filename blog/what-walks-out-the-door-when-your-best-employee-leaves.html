What Walks Out the Door When Your Best Employee Leaves?

By Mark Lucas, founder of JamesTech LLC, Columbia, MD. Roughly thirty years on the Microsoft stack, three of them at Microsoft.

Every firm has one.

The person who knows how everything actually works. Which client gets the exception. Where the accounting workaround lives. Which vendor to call when the system does the weird thing, and why it was set up that way in the first place. None of it is written down, because it never had to be. You could always ask her.

Then she gives notice.

Retirement, a better offer, a move to another department. It doesn't matter which. What matters is the meeting two weeks later, when somebody asks "how did we used to handle this?" and the room goes quiet.

People treat that as an HR problem. I think it's a systems problem, and I think AI has changed the math on solving it.

Your people know more than your systems do

Most 5 to 200 person firms are drowning in information and starving for knowledge. The Word docs are in Microsoft 365. The PDFs are in SharePoint, or on the office server, or both. The spreadsheets are in somebody's OneDrive. The decisions are in email threads from 2022. The procedures are in a personal folder called "Denise stuff."

The firm has all of it. It just can't find any of it without the one person who put it there.

That's institutional knowledge: the accumulated understanding of how the business runs, including the exceptions, the history, the vendor relationships, and the reasons behind decisions nobody would make the same way today. When you don't capture it, the employee becomes the database. And databases that walk out the door are a problem.

"Ask Denise" is not a plan

You've heard it. "Ask Denise, she knows how that works."

Which is fine while Denise sits ten feet away. It stops being fine when she's out sick for a week, and it stops being a business when she retires.

Technology has the same pattern. One person knows the Microsoft 365 tenant. One person understands the one application everyone depends on. One person holds all the vendor contacts and knows why the permissions look the way they do. Good IT people spend their careers removing single points of failure from networks. The same idea applies to what the firm knows.

Critical knowledge should not depend on one person's memory. That's the whole principle, and everything below is how to act on it.

Write it down first

The first fix isn't clever. Document things.

Start with whatever would hurt most if it disappeared tomorrow:

  • The core recurring workflows, the ones that happen every week or every month
  • Client-specific or donor-specific exceptions
  • Vendor contacts, support logins, and what each vendor is for
  • How the key systems are configured, and why
  • Administrative and compliance procedures
  • The troubleshooting steps for the things that break every quarter
  • How a new person gets onboarded and trained
  • Important decisions, and the context that made them the right call at the time

You don't need every keystroke. Write what a competent replacement would need to keep the firm running on Monday. That turns documentation from busywork into business continuity, and it's the same document that later becomes the training manual and the AI's source of truth.

SharePoint is more than a file cabinet

Most firms already own the tool for this. They pay for it monthly and use it as a place to dump files.

SharePoint, set up on purpose, gives procedures and policies and training material a structured home with permissions that make sense. Instead of the same process living in four places in four versions, it lives in one library, with one owner, and one version history.

But buying Microsoft 365 doesn't do that for you. A messy file server moved to the cloud is still a messy file server. The work is in setting it up around how the firm operates: which sites, which libraries, who owns each one, and who can see what. That's most of what I do on the Microsoft 365 side of my practice, and it always comes before the AI.

AI changes what documentation is worth

Documentation always had a catch. Somebody still had to go find it, and most people would rather ask Denise.

That's the part AI actually fixes.

Microsoft Search already finds content across SharePoint, OneDrive, and Exchange while respecting each person's permissions. Copilot Studio can point an agent at a specific SharePoint library and answer questions from it. A Claude project can do the same with a folder of plain files. So a new hire who needs to know how the firm handles a particular client request doesn't dig through folders or interrupt three coworkers. They ask:

What's our process for this?

And the answer comes back from the firm's own approved documents, with a link to the source.

Here's the test I use with clients. Ask any AI a question about your business. Does it answer like a teammate, or like a stranger who met you five seconds ago? A stranger is what you get when the knowledge is in a drawer, an inbox, and one person's head. A teammate is what you get when it's written down where the AI can read it.

Which brings up the catch that replaced the old one: AI can't retrieve what the firm never captured.

AI doesn't fix bad knowledge management

If your procedures are three years out of date, the AI will confidently explain the three-year-old procedure.

If the same process exists in three documents with three different steps, the AI now has three versions to choose from, and it will pick one.

If the critical detail lives only in Denise's head, there's nothing there to find.

This is why I tell people managing AI agents is mostly about what's underneath them: the documents, the permissions, who owns each piece, and what the agent is allowed to touch. AI makes good knowledge management much more valuable. It does nothing for bad knowledge management except make the mess searchable.

Turnover is the real test

Try this. Pick the five people who have been with the firm the longest, or who hold the most specialized knowledge. For each one, ask:

What would we lose if this person didn't come in tomorrow?

Would anyone else know how their key processes work? Where their information is? Which clients have special requirements? How their systems are configured? Who their vendor contacts are? Why certain decisions were made, and what's still half-finished on their desk?

If the honest answer is "we'd figure it out," you've just found a business continuity risk with a name on it.

One nonprofit I'm working with right now keeps its most important tracking list on paper and its history in a filing drawer. Nothing is wrong with the people. The firm has never been asked to survive without them, and it's about to be asked, because two of them are near retirement.

Don't wait for the exit interview

The worst week to start documenting somebody's job is their last one. Knowledge capture should be a habit, not an event.

For critical roles, write down what the person does, how they do it, which systems they touch, who they work with, where their information lives, and who could cover for them. Then store it somewhere the firm owns and controls. Which means not on their desktop, not in their inbox, not in the notebook next to their keyboard, and not in their head. That last one is the scary one.

Where the company knows how

The goal isn't to record every thought an employee has. It's to keep the knowledge the firm depends on from leaving when a person does.

Microsoft 365, SharePoint, search, and AI make that knowledge easier to organize and far easier to find. They work best on top of deliberate documentation, sensible permissions, clear ownership, and someone whose job it is to keep it current. Done right, the payoff goes well past turnover: faster onboarding, better training, quicker troubleshooting, and, eventually, AI agents that answer the way a twenty-year employee would.

Instead of staff asking "who knows how to do this?" they start asking "where does the company know how to do this?" and the answer is a link.

That is what I mean by an AI operating system: the firm's knowledge written down where the AI can read it, under the firm's own control. And once agents are reading it, they need an owner too, which is the agentic AI management side of the work.

What would walk out the door tomorrow?

You don't have to wait for a resignation to find out.

Pick one critical employee and map the knowledge around their role. What do they know that isn't written down? Where is their information stored? Who else has access? Could a coworker find what they need without calling them?

Then do it again with the next person.

The tools to capture, organize, secure, and search institutional knowledge already exist, and most firms already pay for them. The harder step is deciding that the knowledge is an asset worth protecting. Your best people should absolutely be valuable. The firm just shouldn't be fragile because they are.
